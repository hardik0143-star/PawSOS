Practical care guides you can actually use

Open a guide when you need it. Each lesson explains daily care, warning signs, what not to do, and when professional help is appropriate. Guidance is educational and species needs can differ.

Bringing a new animal home: the first 7 days4–5 min guide • settling in safely
＋

Start small and predictable. Give the animal a quiet, secure area with clean water, appropriate food, comfortable bedding or species-appropriate housing, and a place to retreat. Avoid a parade of visitors on day one. For prey species and birds, excessive handling can be especially stressful.

First-week checklist

  • Keep the previous diet initially when possible; sudden diet changes can upset digestion.
  • Introduce other animals gradually and supervise early interactions.
  • Check eating, drinking, stool/urine, breathing, movement and behaviour every day.
  • Arrange an early veterinary relationship and take any records for vaccinations, parasite control, medications and previous illness.
  • Pet-proof the environment: medicines, chemicals, wires, toxic foods/plants, open windows, balconies and unsafe small objects should be inaccessible.
Seek veterinary help promptly for breathing difficulty, collapse, seizures, severe pain, major wounds, repeated vomiting, inability to urinate, suspected poisoning, or a rabbit/bird that suddenly stops eating and appears unwell.
🚑
Emergency first response: what to do before help arrives5 min guide • stabilise, don’t improvise
＋

Your first job is safety: protect yourself from traffic, bites, kicks, claws, wings and frightened-animal behaviour. Call a veterinarian/rescuer early rather than trying multiple home treatments.

Useful first steps

  • Bleeding: apply steady pressure with clean gauze/cloth. Do not repeatedly lift it to check.
  • Heat distress: move to shade/cool air, use room-temperature water and airflow, and seek veterinary help. Avoid ice-cold immersion.
  • Possible poisoning: keep the product/label, note amount/time if known, and call a vet or poison service. Do not induce vomiting unless instructed.
  • Road injury: minimise movement. Small animals may be moved on a firm support/blanket if safe; large animals need trained handling.
  • Birds: minimal handling, a ventilated box, quiet/dim conditions, and prompt avian/wildlife advice. Do not force food or water.
Emergency signs: breathing difficulty, collapse, uncontrolled bleeding, repeated seizures, severe trauma, blue/pale gums, inability to stand, severe abdominal swelling, or suspected toxin exposure.
🥗
Food, body condition & healthy weight5 min guide • dogs and cats
＋

A healthy diet is not just a brand name. It should match species, age/life stage, activity, body condition and medical needs. Use the feeding directions as a starting point, then track the animal—not only the scoop size.

  • Measure food consistently instead of estimating by eye.
  • Keep treats and table extras modest so they do not displace balanced nutrition.
  • Watch weight trends and body condition. Sudden gain/loss or muscle loss deserves attention.
  • Fresh water should be continuously available unless a veterinarian gives different instructions.
  • Pregnancy, growth, senior age, kidney/heart disease, diabetes, obesity and other conditions may require an individual veterinary nutrition plan.

WSAVA provides nutrition-assessment tools, body-condition charts and owner resources designed to support individualized feeding decisions.

Reference: WSAVA Global Nutrition Guidelines ↗

⚠️
Toxic foods & household hazards4 min guide • know what needs a call
＋

Common human foods can be dangerous to pets. Risk depends on species, dose and the individual animal, so a “small amount” should not automatically be assumed safe.

  • Dogs: grapes/raisins, xylitol-containing products, chocolate/caffeine, onions/garlic and alcohol are important hazards.
  • Cats: onions/garlic and many human medicines can be dangerous; cats should not be treated as small dogs.
  • Birds/rabbits: avocado can be particularly hazardous. Species-specific diets matter.
  • Raw meat/eggs can carry pathogens; bones can cause obstruction or injury.
  • Keep medicines, rodenticides, pesticides, cleaners and nicotine products secured.
If exposure may have occurred, note the product, ingredient, approximate amount and time. Contact a veterinarian/poison service promptly and do not induce vomiting unless specifically instructed.

Reference: ASPCA foods to avoid ↗

🛁
Grooming without stress4 min guide • coat, skin, nails & hygiene
＋

Good grooming is health monitoring. Use short, calm sessions and stop if the animal is painful, panicked or struggling hard.

  • Coat/skin: brush according to coat type; look for mats, sores, parasites, lumps, discharge or sudden hair/feather changes.
  • Bathing: use species-appropriate products. Over-bathing may dry skin; birds have different bathing needs from dogs.
  • Nails/claws: trim only when you can identify safe anatomy. If unsure, ask a vet/groomer to demonstrate.
  • Ears: clean only the visible outer area with vet-recommended products; do not insert cotton buds deeply.
  • Teeth: pet-specific dental products only. Persistent bad breath, drooling, difficulty chewing or mouth pain deserves a veterinary exam.

For horses, cattle, buffaloes, goats, sheep and camels, foot/hoof condition and lameness are part of routine care; use an experienced professional when trimming or corrective work is needed.

☀️
Heat, travel & hydration safety4 min guide • hot-weather prevention
＋

Heat stress can become an emergency quickly. Risk is affected by temperature, humidity, ventilation, exercise, breed/body type, age, weight and existing disease.

  • Provide shade, ventilation and reliable clean water.
  • Shift exercise/transport to cooler parts of the day when possible.
  • Never leave an animal in a parked vehicle.
  • During transport, plan water/rest stops and confirm that carriers/vehicles have safe airflow.
  • Large animals need adequate ventilation during loading and transport; avoid overcrowding.
Urgent signs: collapse, marked weakness, confusion, very laboured breathing, inability to stand, or worsening despite cooling. Begin gradual cooling and contact a veterinarian.
🐇
Rabbit essentials: diet, teeth & gut health5 min guide • high-fibre care
＋

Rabbits have a specialised digestive system and need frequent fibre intake. Merck’s owner guidance describes a healthy rabbit diet as predominantly grass hay, with smaller amounts of appropriate pellets and other suitable foods.

  • Provide abundant good-quality grass hay and fresh clean water.
  • Use measured rabbit pellets appropriate for life stage rather than unlimited calorie-dense feeds.
  • Introduce new foods gradually.
  • Chewing fibrous food supports normal tooth wear, but dental disease can still occur.
  • Track appetite and droppings every day; reduced food intake and fewer droppings can be important warning signs.
  • Support the hindquarters when handling; rabbits can injure themselves with forceful struggling.
A rabbit that stops eating, becomes very quiet, develops a swollen abdomen, has markedly fewer/no droppings, or shows obvious pain needs prompt rabbit-experienced veterinary assessment.

Reference: Merck Veterinary Manual — rabbit diet ↗

🐦
Found a wild bird: rescue basics5 min guide • minimise stress
＋

Not every bird on the ground needs rescue. A fully feathered fledgling may still be cared for by nearby parents. A bird that is injured, cold, unable to stand/fly normally, caught by a cat/dog, entangled, or obviously in distress needs advice from a wildlife rehabilitator or avian veterinarian.

  • Keep children and pets away and assess traffic/predator danger first.
  • If containment is necessary and safe, use a ventilated cardboard box lined with paper/towel.
  • Keep the box quiet, dim and away from unnecessary handling.
  • Do not force-feed or pour water into the beak; aspiration can be dangerous.
  • For thread/manja entanglement, do not pull hard if it is embedded or wrapped around a limb/wing—seek experienced help.
Cat/dog attacks can cause hidden punctures and infection even when wounds look small. Seek professional advice promptly.

Further owner education: Association of Avian Veterinarians ↗

🪶
Companion bird daily care & signs of illness6 min guide • avian wellbeing
＋

Birds often hide illness until they are significantly unwell. Know what is normal for your individual bird: activity, vocalisation, appetite, droppings, breathing, posture and feather condition.

Daily care

  • Provide clean water and wash food/water bowls routinely.
  • Use species-appropriate nutrition; a seed-only diet is not appropriate for many companion parrots.
  • Offer safe perches of appropriate diameter/texture and regular opportunities for movement and enrichment.
  • Remove spoiled fresh food promptly and maintain cage/aviary hygiene.
  • Use safe toys and inspect for frayed fibres, small parts, toxic metals or entanglement hazards.

Warning signs

Persistent fluffing, sitting low, reduced eating, major droppings changes, tail-bobbing/effortful breathing, discharge, bleeding, weakness, repeated falling, or a sudden change in normal behaviour warrants an avian-veterinary call.

Reference: AAV Bird Owner Resources ↗

🐄
Large-animal daily health check5 min guide • cattle, buffalo, goats & sheep
＋

Large-animal problems are easier to notice when you know the herd’s normal pattern. Observe animals before entering closely; behaviour from a distance often reveals who is not eating, moving or interacting normally.

  • Check appetite, water access, rumination where applicable, manure/urine and normal herd behaviour.
  • Look for lameness, swollen joints, wounds, abnormal posture, bloating, discharge, coughing or breathing effort.
  • Inspect feet/hooves regularly and keep standing areas reasonably clean and dry.
  • Keep feed changes gradual and avoid sudden access to unfamiliar high-energy feeds.
  • During pregnancy/birth, have a plan for veterinary help before an emergency occurs.
Severe bloat, difficult birth, inability to stand, major trauma, uncontrolled bleeding, severe respiratory distress or sudden neurological signs require urgent veterinary help. Avoid forceful lifting/dragging of a down large animal without trained direction.
🐎
Horse essentials: forage, hooves & colic awareness6 min guide • daily equine care
＋

For many healthy adult horses, good-quality forage and fresh water form the feeding foundation. Concentrates depend on work, body condition, forage quality and life stage; major feed changes should be gradual.

  • Check water, forage intake, manure, movement, legs/hooves and general attitude every day.
  • Arrange regular hoof care with a suitable farrier/hoof professional and investigate persistent lameness.
  • Provide safe turnout/exercise appropriate to the horse.
  • Avoid sudden large grain/concentrate changes; excessive high-starch feeding can raise digestive/metabolic risks.

Know common colic signals

Repeated pawing, looking at the flank, kicking at the abdomen, lying down/rolling, sweating, abdominal distension, reduced appetite or fewer bowel movements can occur with colic. Severity varies, so call a veterinarian rather than attempting to diagnose the cause yourself.

References: Merck horse feeding ↗ · Merck colic guide ↗

🧠
Behaviour, enrichment & humane handling5 min guide • lower stress, better care
＋

Behaviour is communication. Punishing fear, pain or species-normal behaviour can worsen stress and make handling less safe.

  • Learn each species’ warning signals and give retreat space.
  • Use gradual, reward-based training for routine handling where appropriate.
  • Dogs/cats benefit from play, sniffing/exploration, predictable rest and social contact matched to the individual.
  • Rabbits need hiding places, chewing/foraging opportunities and secure exercise space.
  • Birds benefit from safe sensory, nutritional, manipulative, environmental and behavioural enrichment.
  • Horses and herd animals benefit from appropriate movement, foraging/grazing opportunities and social contact when safe.

Sudden aggression, withdrawal or handling intolerance may reflect pain or illness and should not automatically be treated as a “training problem.”

Bird enrichment reference: Association of Avian Veterinarians ↗

🪱
Vaccines, parasites & preventive care4 min guide • build a plan with your vet
＋

Preventive schedules are not identical everywhere. Species, age, local disease risk, indoor/outdoor lifestyle, travel, pregnancy, herd management and previous treatment all matter.

  • Keep a written vaccination record and set reminders before doses are due.
  • Discuss flea/tick/heartworm or other parasite prevention according to local exposure.
  • For livestock/equids, parasite control should be planned around local veterinary guidance and management rather than repeatedly treating without a strategy.
  • Never use a dog/cat parasite product on another species unless a veterinarian confirms it is safe.
  • Regular examinations can catch dental disease, weight changes, skin/hoof problems and chronic disease earlier.
